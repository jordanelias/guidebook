§ 1

What a corridor width is, in a building

Corridor width is a circulation and wayfinding technique. It is not a property a corridor has; it is one answer to a question about moving through a building.

Objective. In the item register this parameter sits in category E, Circulation and Access, alongside lifts, ramps, doors, level entry, slip resistance, rest seating and tactile surface indicators. It links to two functional demands at full and partial strength — wheeled movement and transfer, and ambulant movement — and to a third, balance, at weak strength.

Reasoning. Circulation is the function; a width is one technique serving it. The journey this dimension serves also asks a person to know where they are, stay upright, find and open a door, rest, and not be overloaded on the way. None of those is satisfiable by a width, and each belongs to a different item. Reasoning that stops at the cross-section has followed the technique past the point where the governing variable changed.

The scales below move outward. At each one the parameter that governs is different, which is the practical content of the claim that width is a technique rather than the subject.

Questions this raises

What access need is this specification serving — not which technique does it name? At what scale does the governing variable stop being this one? Who else occupies this space, and what does this dimension take from them? If it takes something away, does another provision restore it, and does that provision exist yet?

§ 2

The figures, and what kind of thing each one is

Sort or filter the table. Select any row to open its sources, pedigree and caveats.

Radial turn and swept path measure different events

A radial turning circle (i.e. the diameter a device is assumed to sweep pivoting about its own centre) is the circle inscribed in a square in most code drawings. A swept path is the floor area actually covered while a device executes a named manoeuvre.

The two are not commensurable. A 1500 mm turning circle can neither corroborate nor contradict a 2400 mm swept envelope, because they describe different physical events. The 1925 mm and 2400 mm figures are both 180° reversals — a U-turn within a bay, not a pivot in place — differing only in whether the bay was enclosed or open-ended, which changes how far a user can lengthen the turn. Real turns are asymmetric: the rotation axis sits at the inner wheel rather than the occupant's centre, and footrests sweep a forward arc the circle excludes.

Where the record does not resolve

The 1800 mm passing width appears in nearly every instrument surveyed and has no traceable measurement behind it. Two readings are available, and the brief carries both:

a. If the convergence reflects an unrecorded common source, then the figure may be sound and the task is to find that source — which would move it from the weak band to a graded one.

b. If there is no common source, then twenty-odd jurisdictions have inherited a figure none of them tested, and the convergence is evidence about regulatory copying rather than about corridors.

Nothing in the record distinguishes (a) from (b). Until it does, the honest grade is the weak band, and the derivation is logged as an open question rather than resolved by preference.

What this means at the drawing board

The figures above are evidence, not a specification. What a drawing needs is the clear dimension that survives everything mounted in it — so the section below shows what subtracts from a nominal structural opening.

A defensible starting point, and what each step down costs

This brief does not prescribe a value. It can say what the record supports and what is given up at each reduction, which is the information a drawing decision actually needs.

  • On a route where signing is anticipatedThe evidence supports ≥ 2440 mm clear. Below it, two people cannot hold a signed conversation while walking; the language is switched off in the corridor. This is the one figure the guidebook advances as a position.
  • Wherever the route turns or admits a roomThe measured swept envelope reaches about 2400 mm for the entire sample. Below it, a powered chair is forced into the high-load spin turn rather than a free turn.
  • Where only two-device passing governs1800 mm is the near-universal code figure — with no traceable measurement behind it. Sound as a floor, weak as a target.
  • Below 1800 mmPassing requires one party to reverse or wait. Treat as a constrained-retrofit condition to document, not a design value.
  • What to check before committing any of theseDoor latch-side clearance and swing, handrail projection, and whether the route turns — each of which can consume the margin the figure was chosen to provide.

§ 3

Swept path, by device class

What was measured, on which devices, and what the study does not cover.

Objective. One study widened a passageway in 5 cm increments until each participant completed a task without touching the walls, across an L-turn, a 360° turn in place, and U-turns with and without a barrier.

The study's stated conclusion

"Between 10% and 100% of users would not be able to maneuver in spaces that meet current Accessibility Guidelines for Buildings and Facilities specifications."

Reasoning. Where a corridor is too narrow to pass without turning, the manoeuvre it forces is a spin turn: one hand brakes on the rim while the other pushes. Peak braking force on the braking arm runs many times a straight push, a wheelchair user makes roughly nine hundred turns a day, and upper-limb injury prevalence among manual users sits between 55 and 72 per cent. On that reading the width is a dose parameter rather than a comfort parameter.

Two limits travel with this. Per-manoeuvre millimetre values sit in the paper's tables behind a paywall and have not been extracted, so they are not reproduced here rather than approximated. The study establishing spin-turn dominance used ten able-bodied novices.

Where the record does not resolve

The device classes above were measured on a fleet assembled before 2010. Two readings follow:

a. If the fleet has not materially changed, the envelopes hold and the only task is extracting the per-task values.

b. If powered chairs and scooters have grown heavier and larger since — which the corpus records as likely — then every figure here understates present need, and the direction of the error is known even though its size is not.

No replacement anthropometric study has been identified. Under (b) the figures remain the best available and are stated with the direction of their error, which is a different claim from stating them as current.

§ 4

Who a wider corridor may hinder

Best practice is not automatically better for everyone.

Objective. Three groups route to the balance demand. The balance research holds twenty-one sources, including handrail-grasp biomechanics, a Cochrane falls review and an international handrail specification.

Who a wider corridor may affect — balance demand

VES · people with vestibular disorders primary BRAIN · people with acquired brain injury primary COM · people with complex conditions situational · presyncope

Who the same width serves — and would lose by narrowing

MOB · wheelchair users passing and swept turn SCI · people with spinal cord injuries injury dose BAR · people in larger bodies width envelope DEAF · Deaf and hard-of-hearing people signing pair DEAFBLIND · DeafBlind people intervenor pair

Reasoning. Someone who walks unsteadily may cross a corridor by staying within reach of something solid. Widen the route and the wall moves away. Narrowing is not the resolution: it re-imposes the injury-dose turn on wheelchair users, trading one group's shoulders for another's balance. The candidate answer is continuous support at full width — but a handrail deducts about 50 mm from clear width, and no handrail-dimension item exists to carry it.

Scope of the claim. Balance is well evidenced. What no source addresses is the interaction: how far a steadying surface may sit from a walking person before the distance is itself the barrier. Handrails are studied on stairs; corridor width is studied for wheeled passage and signing. Nobody has measured them against each other.

Who the contrast provisions pull apart

BLIND · blind and low-vision people needs high contrast at junctions DEM · people living with dementia needs low floor differential VES · people with vestibular disorders needs plain, non-patterned floor

Contrast shows the same shape. High luminance contrast at junctions helps a low-vision traveller read an edge; low contrast between adjacent floor materials stops a tonal change being misread as a hole; plain flooring avoids optic-flow patterns that provoke visual vertigo. All three are evidenced, which makes "high contrast is good practice" false as a universal.

Where the record does not resolve

On the width-versus-balance question the record supports two readings, and this brief asserts neither:

a. If steadying distance matters at corridor scale, then wide primary routes owe a continuous handrail, and the missing item is urgent rather than tidy-up.

b. If it does not — if people who need support use the wall opportunistically and a wide route simply changes where they walk — then the tension dissolves and the handrail question belongs to stairs and ramps alone.

The measurement that would separate these has not been made. Anyone citing this brief for either position is citing a question, not a finding.

§ 6

What the record cannot yet say

Two limits in the guidebook's own machinery, which is why trade-offs stay invisible.

The database cannot record harm. An item linked to a functional demand can be marked full, partial or weak — three degrees of serving. No value means "increasing this parameter degrades that demand." The table built to hold conflicts between groups exists, with the right columns, and holds no rows.

The search protocol did not look for harm. Generated queries asked what a provision achieves and, adversarially, whether a source was weak. Neither asked who a design fails. Harm-oriented searching happened anyway, slug by slug, untooled and in one language.

A withheld row and an unwritten row look identical. Neither is queryable and neither reaches a reader, so the belief that best practice is universally better survives without anyone asserting it.
