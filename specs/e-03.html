Ramp Gradient

Can someone with MS-related fatigue use this ramp without it exhausting them before they reach the top?

How steep a ramp may be, and how far it can run before a rest. Most codes cap the slope at 1:12; the Guidebook position is 1:20.

1What to specify

Everyone

EveryoneNo steeper than 1:20 where space allows.

The value in the Guidebook's item register (E-03). The clinical derivation is not yet supported inside the library: the study named as its basis (gap GAP-019) is a backrest-height trial at a single 1:19.1 slope. Treat as a working position, not a finding.

Legal maximum, most codes1:12

1:12 is the most common legal maximum (US, UK, Japan, New Zealand, Canada, Brazil and others). See the jurisdiction comparison. A legal maximum is a regulatory floor, not a best-practice value.

Specific groups

Manual wheelchair usersNo steeper than 1:20.

Studies: a 36-person study of wheelchair users with spinal cord injury on level vs a 1:19.1 slope (higher force uphill), a ramp slope/height/pushing-force study (abstract not retrieved), a 34-study review, and a 171-person trial in which most could climb 1:8 over 9.14 m. None derives a threshold.

Sources:

mobility impairment (general) · Low-effort movement · Step-free route · Space to manoeuvre, turn and pass

Fatigue-limited conditions (e.g. MS)No steeper than 1:20, with places to rest.

This is the mechanism named in the item's title. Studies of people with multiple sclerosis show higher energy cost of walking and higher perceived uphill effort; they do not identify a slope threshold.

Sources:

multiple sclerosis · orthostatic/fatigue spectrum · Low-effort movement · Rest and recovery opportunities

One person

One personSet by individual assessment

No general value. The usable slope and length depend on the person's strength, stamina, equipment and the length of the route, so they are set by individual assessment (for example with an occupational therapist) and trial on the actual route. Design at this scale is individual by definition. Evidence on how much people differ: in the 171-person trial the range of tolerated slopes was wide.

2Why slope matters

  • Steeper ramps take more effort. Wheelchair users pushing up a 1:19.1 slope used more force, torque and cadence than on the level (36 users with spinal cord injury).
  • The effort lands on the arms and shoulders. A 34-study review found slope effects under-studied.
  • People with multiple sclerosis spend more energy walking, and uphill feels harder than the measured cost suggests.
  • Going down matters too. At 1:12 and 1:8 most users coped, but power-chair users and people with vision impairment raised safety concerns on steeper descents.
  • Climbing a short ramp is not the same as managing it all day. In a 171-person trial only a few manual wheelchair users could not climb 1:8 over 9.14 m; it tested one ramp, not sustained use.
  • Most codes cap running slope at 1:12; many allow that only for short runs and prefer 1:20 or gentler.

United Kingdom1:20 (going up to 10 m); 1:12 at most, for goings up to 2 m● primary source read

Approved Document M, Volume 2: Buildings other than dwellings (England), 1.19-1.26 Ramped access; Table 1; Diagram 3, 2015 edition incorporating 2020 and 2024 amendments

Gradient by going (Table 1)
1:20 for a going up to 10 m (rise up to 500 mm); 1:15 up to 5 m (rise up to 333 mm); 1:12 up to 2 m (rise up to 166 mm). Interpolate between: 1:14 for a 4 m going, 1:19 for 9 m.
Limits per flight
No flight with a going over 10 m or a rise over 500 mm. A lift or other alternative for wheelchair users when the total rise is over 2 m. Stated reason: a high rise is unacceptably tiring for wheelchair users and some people with walking difficulties, even with rest landings.
Design intent
Gradients should be as shallow as practicable: steep gradients are hard for wheelchair users who lack the strength to climb or to slow down. Some ambulant disabled people find ramps harder than steps; people may need to stop frequently to regain strength or breath or ease pain.
Width and handrails
Surface width between walls, upstands or kerbs at least 1.5 m; handrail on both sides (people with one-sided weakness need support on both).
Landings
Foot and head landing at least 1.2 m long, clear of door swings; intermediate landings at least 1.5 m long; passing places 1.8 m by 1.8 m where the far end is not visible or there are three flights or more. Landings level: no steeper than 1:60 along their length, cross-fall no steeper than 1:40.
Edge protection and surface
Kerb at least 100 mm high on any open side, contrasting visually. Ramp surface slip-resistant when wet and in a colour that contrasts with the landings; similar friction on ramp and landings.
Stepped route as well
Clearly sign-posted steps are provided in addition when the rise of the ramp is over 300 mm (two 150 mm steps).
Internal ramps
A section of an internal route with a gradient of 1:20 or steeper is designed as a ramp to Table 1 and Diagram 3; level means no steeper than 1:60.

Compared with the Guidebook position

The preferred gradient matches the Guidebook position (1:20) but only for goings up to 10 m; 1:12 is allowed for goings up to 2 m.

Ireland1:20 preferred (up to 10 m); 1:12 only for flights up to 2 m● primary source read

Building Regulations Technical Guidance Document M: Access and Use (2022), 1.1.3.4 Ramped access routes; Table 2; 2.1.3.4 existing buildings, 2022

Gradient by going (Table 2)
1:20 for a flight up to 10 m (rise up to 500 mm); 1:15 up to 5 m (rise up to 333 mm); 1:12 up to 2 m (rise up to 166 mm). Interpolation is allowed between 2 m and 10 m.
Preferred vs maximum
Preferred maximum 1:20. Ramps not steeper than 1:12 only where site constraints require it and each flight is no longer than 2 m. Stated reason: a person using or pushing a wheelchair may not have the strength to climb a steeper or longer flight, and there is a danger of falling forwards going down or backwards going up.
What counts as a ramp
A gradient of 1:20 or steeper is a ramp. A gradient steeper than 1:50 but gentler than 1:20 is a gently sloped route; 1:20 or gentler is preferred.
Clear width and handrails
Clear width between walls, upstands or kerbs at least 1.5 m; at least 1.2 m between handrails; handrails on both sides. A wide ramp is divided by extra handrails into channels 1.2 m to 2.5 m wide.
Landings
Level landing (1:50 or gentler counts as level) at top and bottom, at least 1.8 m long by 1.8 m wide and clear of door swings; intermediate landings at least 1.5 m long, and 1.8 m by 1.8 m as passing places where the far end of the ramp cannot be seen or there are three flights or more.
Edge protection
Upstand at least 100 mm high on any open side of a ramp or landing, contrasting visually with the surface.
Stepped route as well
Where the rise is more than 300 mm, a stepped route must be provided in addition to the ramp. Stated reason: some people find steps easier than a ramp, such as people with mobility difficulties who use certain walking aids, older people and people with vision impairment.
Alternative means of access
A platform lift or similar where the ramp is 1:20 or steeper and the total rise is more than 2 m. Stated reason: ramps become too tiring for wheelchair users beyond that height.
Existing buildings (2.1.3.4)
Where the full figures are not practicable: 1:12 flights up to 4.5 m (interpolating from 1:20 at 10 m); width between handrails at least 1 m; top and bottom landings at least 1.5 m by 1.5 m; intermediate landings at least 1 m long.

Compared with the Guidebook position

The preferred gradient matches the Guidebook position (1:20). The 1:12 maximum is confined to flights of 2 m or less.

Singapore1:12 for a rise over 175 mm; run limited to 6 m at that gradient● primary source read

BCA Code on Accessibility in the Built Environment 2025 (v1.1), 4.5 Changes in levels and kerb ramps; 4.6 Ramps; Tables 4 and 5, 2025 (version 1.1, revised 1 March 2026)

Gradient by rise (Table 4)
Not steeper than: 1:2 for a rise of 0 to 15 mm; 1:5 for more than 15 mm to 50 mm; 1:10 for more than 50 mm to 175 mm; 1:12 for a rise over 175 mm. The gradient must be consistent between landings.
Run between landings (Table 5)
Maximum run: 6 m at 1:12; 9 m at 1:14; 12 m at 1:16; 15 m at 1:20; 18 m at 1:24. Colour contrast, handrails and edge protection are mandatory at 1:12 and 1:14; the table marks them non-mandatory for gentler ramps.
Landings
Level top and bottom landing for each run and at changes of direction, at least 1.5 m deep and as wide as the ramp.
Width
As for accessible routes (Table 3). May reduce to 1.2 m where a stepped alternative runs beside the ramp and the total rise is no more than 1.2 m.
Handrails
On both sides for a ramp run with a rise over 175 mm, 800 mm to 900 mm high, continuous, with extensions of at least 300 mm beyond top and bottom. Not required at 1:25 or gentler.
Edge protection
Kerb at least 75 mm high, or a barrier or rail whose lower edge is 75 mm or less above the surface, where a ramp or landing is not beside a wall.
Curved ramps and cross-fall
Curved ramps 1:16 or gentler. Cross-fall for drainage no steeper than 1:40.
Visibility of the change in level
For a rise of 15 mm or more, a permanent contrasting colour or a band at least 300 mm deep at the top and bottom. Not required at 1:25 or gentler.
Kerb ramps (rise up to 175 mm)
Not steeper than 1:10 including flared sides; clear width at least 900 mm; slip-resistant.

Compared with the Guidebook position

The maximum gradient (1:12) is steeper than the Guidebook position (1:20); a 1:20 ramp may run 15 m between landings.
