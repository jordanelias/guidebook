Why it matters

  • Evidence in the librarySteeper ramps take more effort to climb. Manual wheelchair users pushing up a 1:19.1 slope produced significantly higher force, torque and cadence than on the level (36 users with spinal cord injury). REF-00388
  • Evidence in the libraryThat effort lands on the arms and shoulders. A 2022 systematic review of 34 studies on wheelchair propulsion over slopes, cross-slopes, kerbs and ground types found the topic under-studied and asked researchers to report ramp length and grade consistently so studies can be compared. REF-00037
  • Evidence found outside the library (abstract level)People with multiple sclerosis use more energy to walk than people without it, and that extra cost is weakly linked to fatigue (meta-analysis of 9 studies). In one uphill-walking study, 18 people with MS rated uphill effort higher than controls even though their measured oxygen cost was not higher, so how hard a slope feels may not track what it costs.
  • Evidence found outside the library (abstract level)Going down matters as well as going up. In a transit-ramp trial with 27 users, 1:12 and 1:8 slopes were acceptable to most, but power-chair users and people with vision impairment raised safety concerns about descending steeper slopes.
  • Evidence found outside the library (abstract level)Being able to climb a short ramp is not the same as managing it all day. A trial of 171 people on 1:8 to 1:20 slopes over 30 ft (9.14 m) found only a few manual wheelchair users could not complete an ascent, even at 1:8. It measured feasibility on one ramp, not repeated or sustained use.
  • Regulatory factLegal limits are a floor, not a finding. Most codes cap running slope at 1:12; many lower it for longer runs. Agreement between codes shows what regulators accepted, not what the evidence supports.

The value in the Guidebook's item register (E-03). The clinical derivation is not yet supported inside the library: the study named as its basis (gap GAP-019) is a backrest-height trial at a single 1:19.1 slope. Treat as a working position, not a finding.

Legal ceiling

1:12 is the most common legal maximum (US, UK, Japan, New Zealand, Canada, Brazil and others).

See the jurisdiction comparison. A legal maximum is a regulatory floor, not a best-practice value.

For one person tailored through individual assessment

One specific person

Set by assessment

No general value. The usable slope and length depend on the person's strength, stamina, equipment and the length of the route, so they are set by individual assessment (for example with an occupational therapist) and trial on the actual route.

Design at this scale is individual by definition. Evidence on how much people differ: in the 171-person trial the range of tolerated slopes was wide.

Evidence found but not yet in the library

  • Sanford, Story & Jones (1997). An analysis of the effects of ramp slope on people with mobility impairments. Assistive Technology. doi:10.1080/10400435.1997.10132293
    171 people, slopes 1:8 to 1:20, 30 ft ramp: only a few manual wheelchair users could not ascend, even at 1:8; no change to ADA slope or length recommended.
    Counter-evidence to treating 1:20 as a feasibility limit for wheelchair users.
  • Lenker et al. (2016). Usability evaluation of access ramps in transit buses. Journal of Public Transportation. DOI not yet resolved
    27 adults; 1:8 and 1:12 usable and acceptable to most; 1:4 too steep; safety concerns about steep descent among power-chair users and people with vision impairment.
    Descent and mixed-user evidence.
  • Rooney et al. (2021). Oxygen cost of walking in people with multiple sclerosis and its association with fatigue. International Journal of MS Care. DOI not yet resolved
    Meta-analysis, 9 studies: higher oxygen cost of walking in MS; weak association with fatigue.
    Supports the energy mechanism named in the item.
  • Har-Nir et al. (2022/23). Energy expenditure and perceived effort during uphill and downhill walking in people with multiple sclerosis. Eur J Phys Rehabil Med. doi:10.23736/s1973-9087.22.07682-1
    18 people with MS: perceived uphill effort higher than controls although oxygen cost was not higher.
    Partly contradicts a pure energy-conservation rationale.
  • Liu et al. (2023). Comparison of gait between walking up and down an incline with a walking device in older adults. Geriatric Nursing. DOI not yet resolved
    26 older adults; cane vs rolling walker differ on incline.
    Ambulant mobility-aid users.
