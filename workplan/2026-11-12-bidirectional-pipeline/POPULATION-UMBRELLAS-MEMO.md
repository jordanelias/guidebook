## 0. Verdict

**Q1. Does "mobility" make sense as an identity population? No.** In the sources retrieved, disabled people do not organise as "people with mobility needs". They organise across disability (Council of Canadians with Disabilities, ENIL), by condition or impairment (spinal cord injury, spina bifida and hydrocephalus, limb loss and limb difference), and by device ("all wheelchair users", United Spinal Association). "Mobility" is the name of a functional domain (ICF chapter d4, the Washington Group's walking question, CRPD Article 20 on personal mobility as a right). `MOB` conflates four different kinds of fact: a function, a set of impairments, the use of an assistive device, and a number of separate communities. The owner's decomposition is correct, but its branches are not all identities: aid type and power-versus-manual are **device** facts; paraplegia-versus-quadriplegia is an **impairment-level** fact; only some branches (limb loss and limb difference, spinal cord injury, wheelchair users) correspond to communities that organise under that name. The design parameters the project has actually researched depend on the device and its user's function, not on a community.

**Q2. Do contemporary advocates think like this? Not in this form.** They hold several layers at once: a cross-disability political identity ("disabled people", "nothing about us without us"); specific communities named on their own terms, some of which reject the impairment frame altogether (Deaf people as a linguistic and cultural group; users and survivors of psychiatry rather than "people with mental health conditions"); device-defined constituencies whose edges are contested (ambulatory wheelchair users); and, separately, functional categories that statisticians use and that advocates treat as tools, not identities. Broad impairment umbrellas ("mobility", "neurological", "mental health") are the administrative layer, not the self-defined one.

**Q3. What do modern ethical approaches propose?** They converge on three points and disagree on a fourth. (1) Classify the **environment's demand and the barrier**, not the person (social model, CRPD, ICF environmental factors, universal design). (2) Where people are named, **they name themselves**, through their own organisations (CRPD Art. 4.3 and General Comment No. 7, epistemic justice, disability justice's "leadership of those most impacted"). (3) Keep the **individual** in view: categories are retrieval aids, never portraits (capabilities approach, care ethics, crip critique, the project's own irreducibility principle). They disagree on (4) whether impairment-specific categories should exist at all: disability justice and the social model press toward cross-disability solidarity and warn about hierarchies of impairment; community self-definition and the capabilities approach press toward specificity. The reconciliation the evidence supports is to keep the two jobs apart: communities for **voice and provenance**, access needs / ICF / device for **design parameters**.

**RECOMMENDATION (the owner decides).** Keep the four lens columns exactly as ruled on 2026-08-28. Change what the **identity** lens is allowed to contain: only communities that disabled people have organised and named themselves, each row carrying the organisation or ethical source it rests on as provenance (the fourteenth statement's own ACTION 2). Retire `MOB` as an identity; hold "mobility" where it already lives, as ICF d4 codes and access needs. Hold device in `device_class` and nowhere else. Hold family resemblance (the "mobility family", the "neurodivergent" coalition) as a parent relation in data, used for navigation and for finding commonality, never as the key of a determination unless the family passes the ratified design-equivalence test. Record who co-produced a source as a typed relation to a community, separately from whom a determination serves. This is option 2 with option 3's hierarchy held as data, and option 4's separation of provenance from the key (section 7).

**Confidence.** High (about 85%) that `MOB` is not an identity and that device and impairment level are not identities. Moderate (about 70%) that restricting the identity lens to self-organised communities is the right rule. Low to moderate (about 50%) on the particular list of communities, because my sample of organisations is international-umbrella and Anglophone (Canada, United States, United Kingdom, Europe), no non-English national organisation was retrieved, and several key organisations could not be reached (section 10).

**What would change it.** (a) Evidence that organisations *of* physically disabled people, as such, are a common form outside English (the United Kingdom's Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation, 1975, is one; I did not retrieve any current national federation of this kind, and I think it likely that several exist: UNVERIFIED). That would support a "physical disability" community row, though still not "mobility". (b) Co-1 evidence that a design parameter varies by community membership after device and function are held fixed. (c) The owner ruling that the identity lens is meant as a reader's entry point rather than as a statement about communities, in which case administrative umbrellas are tolerable as entry aliases, provided they key nothing.

## 2. The lens: how disabled people's own organisations define their constituencies

### 2.1 What counts as an organisation of disabled people

VERIFIED from the United Nations document CRPD/C/GC/7, *General comment No. 7 (2018) on the participation of persons with disabilities, including children with disabilities, through their representative organizations, in the implementation and monitoring of the Convention*, Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, distributed 9 November 2018 (https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3899396/files/CRPD_C_GC_7-EN.pdf):

- Para 11: organisations of persons with disabilities "can only be those that are led, directed and governed by persons with disabilities. A clear majority of their membership should be recruited among persons with disabilities themselves."
- Para 11(d): "They may represent one or more constituencies based on actual or perceived impairment or can be open to membership of all persons with disabilities". Para 11(e): they "can include constituencies based on transversal identities (for example, children, women or indigenous people with disabilities) and comprise members with various impairments".
- Para 12 names the types: umbrella organisations (which "only speak on behalf of their member organizations" and "cannot represent individual persons with disabilities"); cross-disability organisations; self-advocacy organisations, "especially persons with intellectual disabilities"; organisations including family members, for people with intellectual disabilities, dementia and children, whose role "should be to assist and empower"; organisations of women and girls; organisations of children and young persons. Para 12(a) adds: "persons with disabilities should be able to decide for themselves which organizations they want to represent them."
- Para 13: they "should be distinguished from organizations 'for' persons with disabilities, which provide services and/or advocate on behalf of persons with disabilities, which, in practice, may result in a conflict of interests".
- Para 50 gives the Committee's own list of who must be reached. It is not a taxonomy and mixes kinds freely: women, older persons, children, "those requiring high levels of support", victims of landmines, migrants, "persons with actual or perceived psychosocial impairments, persons with intellectual disabilities, neurodiverse persons, including those with autism or dementia, persons with albinism, permanent physical impairments, chronic pain, leprosy and visual impairments and persons who are deaf, deafblind or otherwise hearing-impaired".

Three things follow for a taxonomy. INFERRED from the paragraphs above. (i) The Committee accepts **three bases of constituency at once**: impairment ("actual or perceived"), open cross-disability membership, and transversal identity. It does not rank them. (ii) Constituency is **self-chosen**: the test is who leads and whom people choose to represent them, not which box a classifier puts them in. (iii) An umbrella speaks only for its members on agreed matters and cannot represent individuals. That is the Committee's version of this project's own anti-umbrella rule.

## 3. Q1 in full: `MOB`

### 3.1 What "mobility" is

"Mobility" names an **activity domain**. VERIFIED in the project's own `identity_icf_map`: every ICF code mapped to `MOB` is a d-code (activities), and the mechanism text is "ambulation, balance, transfer, wheelchair use", four different things. VERIFIED from the Washington Group page: its Short Set deliberately asks about "difficulty functioning in six basic, universal actions", using "the ICF as a framework, focusing on the component of activity limitations", and describes this as "a break from the past and the medicalization of disability that placed disability within the person". The six domains themselves are listed in the questionnaire, which I did not retrieve; that walking is one of them is UNVERIFIED here. VERIFIED from the United Nations text of CRPD Article 20: "personal mobility" is a right to be secured, with access to "mobility aids, devices and assistive technologies", not a class of person.

So `MOB` treats the name of a function and of a right as the name of a people. That is the category error.

### 3.4 What the design parameters actually depend on

- **Turning and manoeuvring space depends on the device and its design features.** VERIFIED from the abstract (full text not retrieved) of Koontz et al., "Design Features That Affect the Maneuverability of Wheelchairs and Scooters", *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, 2010: with manual wheelchair, power wheelchair and scooter users, "Ultralight MWCs with rear axles posterior to the shoulder ... required the least amount of space"; "Mid-wheel-drive PWCs required the least space for the 360 degrees-turn in place compared with front-wheel-drive and rear-wheel-drive PWCs"; "PWCs with seat functions required more space"; and "Between 10% and 100% of users would not be able to maneuver in spaces that meet current Accessibility Guidelines". The grouping variable in the analysis is the device's design, not the person's diagnosis or community. (https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:16a1826f-b806-46c7-97fe-171ee59a2273/?utm_source=claude_desktop). The ledger's 2026-09-11 entry quotes this paper's sample line, so the project already holds it; whether it is an admitted source I did not check.
- **Clear floor area depends on occupied device length and width.** VERIFIED from abstracts: D'Souza et al., *Transportation Research Record*, 2010, and Steinfeld et al., *Assistive Technology*, 2010, report current dimensions "inadequate for accommodating many users of wheeled mobility devices, especially those that use power chairs and scooters".
- **Diagnosis does not fix the device.** VERIFIED from the abstract of Martin Ginis et al., *Disability and Rehabilitation*, 2015: among 347 adults with spinal cord injury, those who are ambulatory and those who use manual wheelchairs are distinct subgroups; ambulators are "an often-overlooked subgroup". So `SCI` does not imply a wheelchair, and a wheelchair does not imply `SCI`.
- **Device users sit between categories.** VERIFIED from the abstract of Jang et al., *Disability & Society*, 2019: scooter users experience "liminality", arising partly from their "ambulatory status".
- **Reach depends on seated posture and on trunk and arm function; ramp effort depends on propulsion mode, strength and endurance; handrail and stair use depend on balance and gait.** INFERRED from the access-need definitions in the project's own `access_needs` table and from the ledger's 2026-08-25 entry (ramp gradient opposed between a manual wheelchair user and an ambulant person with fatigue). I retrieved no source on reach or ramp effort in this pass.

**Conclusion for Q1.** For the spatial parameters the corpus holds, the variables that move the number are, in order: the device (class, footprint, drive), the user's function with that device (propulsion, trunk and arm function, endurance), and body size. Community membership moves none of them directly. Community membership matters for a different reason: it says *whose knowledge* the figure came from and *who must be asked* (section 5).

## 4. Q2 in full: do advocates and communities organise this way?

**(a) Cross-disability political identity.** VERIFIED. UPIAS (1975): "it is society which disables physically impaired people. Disability is something imposed on top of our impairments"; "Disabled people are therefore an oppressed group". ENIL: "a user-led network of disabled people", "pan disability". CCD: "of people with all disabilities". ASAN: "Nothing About Us, Without Us!" and "We are One Community". Sins Invalid: "Commitment to Cross-Disability Solidarity ... isolation undermines collective liberation". At this layer the meaningful category is "disabled people", defined by shared exclusion, and impairment type is deliberately not the organising principle.

**(b) Impairment- or condition-specific communities.** VERIFIED for spinal cord injury (rows 15, 16), limb loss and limb difference (18), spina bifida and hydrocephalus (9), autism (19), dementia (20), intellectual disability (7, 21), Down syndrome (8), ME and Long COVID (24), blindness (5), deafblindness (6). These are specific. Not one is as broad as "mobility", "neurological" or "sensory". Cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis (beyond row 25, which is flagged), muscular dystrophy and polio: organisations exist, I believe, but none was retrieved and confirmed as led by disabled people: UNVERIFIED.

**(c) Device-defined identities.** VERIFIED: "wheelchair user" is a constituency term used by a membership organisation (row 16) and an access description at the same time. It is contested at its edge by ambulatory wheelchair users, who are stigmatised as "faking" (row 17 and the McKendrick account). The project's `AMB`/`WHEEL` cut puts those people on both sides of the line or on neither, as the ledger entry itself says. A second assistance-defined constituency exists in Canada: "People who use Guide and Service Dogs" (row 12). INFERRED: device or assistance use is a legitimate basis for a community row, but because a device is also a physical fact that the extraction records, the two must not be the same cell (rule 5; section 7).

**(d) Administrative and statistical categories.** VERIFIED: the Washington Group's functional domains were built for "comparable data cross-nationally" and to test "whether persons with disability participate to the same extent as persons without disabilities". The page states its own limits: the set will "represent the majority of, but not all, persons with limitation in basic actions", and environmental barriers and participation are "extramural". INFERRED: a functional domain is a sound basis for a design guide's *functional* lens and a poor basis for an *identity* lens, because nobody belongs to a walking-difficulty community. Census "ambulatory difficulty" and benefits categories: not retrieved.

**(e) Critiques of impairment-category taxonomies.**

- *Hierarchy of impairments.* VERIFIED from abstracts: Deal, "Disabled people's attitudes toward other impairment groups: a hierarchy of impairments", *Disability & Society*, 2003: disabled people "do not always wish to be associated with other impairment groups", and research is needed on whether disabled people see themselves "as a homogenous in-group, as a set of separate impairment groups, or viewing impairment as only one facet of identity". Deal's 2006 thesis (City University London) reports a rank ordering among disabled respondents, from most to least accepted: "Deaf, Arthritis, Epilepsy, Cerebral Palsy, HIV/AIDS, Down's Syndrome and Schizophrenia". A classifier that sorts by impairment risks reproducing that ranking in coverage and attention. INFERRED.
- *Deaf people as a linguistic and cultural group.* VERIFIED: WFD's mission is "linguistic rights and human rights"; CRPD Article 30(4) recognises "their specific cultural and linguistic identity, including sign languages and deaf culture". VERIFIED from abstracts: Lane, "Ethnicity, Ethics, and the Deaf-World", *Journal of Deaf Studies and Deaf Education*, 2005, argues against construing the Deaf-World as a disability group ("Deaf people themselves do not believe they have a disability"); Ferri et al., *International and Comparative Law Quarterly*, 2024, find three legal approaches in European Union states (minority, cultural, disability) and note deaf advocacy organisations "eschewing the notion of disability" while WFD itself works inside IDA and the CRPD. The community disagrees with itself here, and the taxonomy should not settle it for them.
- *Neurodiversity.* VERIFIED: ASAN defines the neurodiversity movement as concerning "people with brain-based disabilities (like autism, intellectual disabilities, learning disabilities, or mental health disabilities)", and General Comment No. 7 para 50 writes "neurodiverse persons, including those with autism or dementia". So "neurodivergent" is, by the movement's and the Committee's own usage, a coalition term that spans what the registry codes as `AUT`, `ADHD`, `ID`, `MH` and arguably `DEM`. ASAN also rejects functioning labels ("ASAN doesn't use labels like 'high-functioning' and 'low-functioning'"), which is a warning against severity sub-codes.
- *Users and survivors of psychiatry; mad studies.* VERIFIED: the international organisation names itself by experience of the psychiatric system and by "psychosocial disabilities" (row 10). VERIFIED from abstracts: Davar, *Indian Journal of Gender Studies*, 2008, argues that "A mental illness language has been exhausted of any positive content" and that the rights orientation comes "from disability thinking"; Aubrecht, *Review of Disability Studies*, 2012, examines "the imposition of a language of mental illness". "Mad pride" organisations themselves were not retrieved (MindFreedom returned 404).
- *Access needs and access intimacy.* VERIFIED: Mia Mingus, "Access Intimacy: The Missing Link", *Leaving Evidence*, 5 May 2011 (https://leavingevidence.wordpress.com/2011/05/05/access-intimacy-the-missing-link/): "Access intimacy is that elusive, hard to describe feeling when someone else 'gets' your access needs"; "It doesn't mean that our access looks the same". The unit here is the access need of a person in a relationship, not a category of person. The project's `access_needs` lens is the nearest thing it has to this vocabulary. INFERRED.
- *Epistemic justice.* VERIFIED from abstracts: Scully, *International Journal of Feminist Approaches to Bioethics*, 2018, on "the ascription of a global epistemic incapacity to people affected by impairment"; Chapman et al., *Journal of Social Philosophy*, 2022, on hermeneutical injustice, where "a community's shared vocabularies have been structured in a way that unfairly distorts or stifles understanding"; Felder, *Theory and Research in Education*, 2025, on participatory disability research as "a critique and a corrective".
- *Chronic illness, "Spoonie" and fatigue communities; crip theory texts; Protactile and DeafBlind cultural sources; blind-led national federations.* Not retrieved (section 10).

## 5. Q3 in full: what the ethical approaches propose, weighted by the owner's lens

The fourteenth statement puts advocacy groups and disability ethics first. I therefore weight participation and epistemic justice first (who gets to define a category), then the approaches that say what a built-environment guide should classify, then the rest. Sources are those already cited; where an approach's primary texts were not retrieved, that is said.

| Approach | What it holds (source) | What it implies for this taxonomy |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Participation / CRPD Art. 4.3 / Co-1** | States "shall closely consult with and actively involve persons with disabilities ... through their representative organizations" (CRPD Art. 4(3), VERIFIED); organisations are those "led, directed and governed by persons with disabilities" and people "decide for themselves which organizations they want to represent them" (GC7, VERIFIED) | A category of people is legitimate when the people in it chose it. An identity row needs a named community source. A row with none is an administrative label and should not sit in the identity lens. |
| **2. Epistemic justice** | Testimonial and hermeneutical injustice (Scully 2018; Chapman et al. 2022; abstracts) | Naming a group with a word it does not use is a hermeneutical harm; discounting a Co-1 source because its authors do not fit a code is a testimonial one. The registry should store the community's own name and its source, and the project should expect names to change. |
| **3. Disability justice and intersectionality** | Intersectionality; "Leadership of those most impacted"; "Commitment to cross-disability solidarity"; "Collective access" (Sins Invalid, VERIFIED) | Against single-axis sorting of people: one person holds several identities, and race, gender, age and Indigeneity cut across all of them (GC7 11(e) agrees). Supports many-to-many links, never one code per person or per figure. Pulls *against* impairment-specific codes becoming silos. |
| **4. Social model** | Disability is "imposed on top of our impairments" by social organisation (UPIAS 1975, VERIFIED) | Classify barriers and environmental demands, not bodies. | **5. Human-rights model / CRPD** | Persons with disabilities "include those who have long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments which in interaction with various barriers may hinder" participation (Art. 1, VERIFIED): an open, interactional description, not a list. Universal design "shall not exclude assistive devices for particular groups of persons with disabilities where this is needed" (Art. 2, VERIFIED). | **11. This is an argument for the determination key to be needs / ICF / device, with identity as an optional qualifier. |

**Where they conflict, plainly.**

1. **Self-definition versus design equivalence.** Approaches 1 and 2 say use the community's category. The project's own rule says group by shared design demand. They diverge: the spinal cord injury community includes ambulatory people and wheelchair users (Martin Ginis et al.); wheelchair users include many conditions. Neither grouping can do the other's job. This is the central finding, and it is why the recommendation gives the two jobs to two different lenses.
2. **Cross-disability solidarity versus specificity.** Disability justice and the social model resist impairment silos; community organisations and the hierarchy-of-impairment evidence show both that specific communities are real and that sorting by impairment can rank people.
3. **Deaf as linguistic minority versus disabled.** Lane's position and WFD's practice differ; the CRPD holds both (Art. 30(4) inside a disability convention).
4. **Identity-first versus person-first.** Not resolvable in general; it differs by community (section 2.3, point 5). The project's rule already defers to each community; keep it.
5. **"Neurodivergent" versus "autistic", and versus "mental health".** The coalition term includes mental health disabilities by ASAN's definition; users and survivors of psychiatry organise separately and under a different word. A taxonomy that makes `NDV`, `AUT` and `MH` three flat peers hides that one is a coalition spanning the other two.
6. **Diagnosis-optional versus diagnosis-required.** The project's membership doctrine is diagnosis-optional, and ASAN includes self-identified autistic people; Dementia Alliance International makes a confirmed diagnosis a condition of membership. Communities differ on this too.
7. **ICF universalism versus community.** The ICF can describe everyone and names no one. Using it alone would satisfy the social model and fail approaches 1 to 3.

### RECOMMENDATION (for the owner's decision; nothing here is decided)

**Option 2, with option 3's hierarchy held as data, and option 4's separation of provenance from the key.** In the owner's own terms:

1. **Leave the four lens columns as ruled.** No change to the 2026-08-28 key.
2. **Rule what the identity lens may contain: a community that disabled people organised and named themselves.** Each row carries the source it rests on (an organisation of disabled people, a governing document, or a Co-1 publication) and the date read. This is the fourteenth statement's ACTION 2 applied to the registry, and the 2026-07-22 rule ("specific self-identified communities named on their own terms") finally enforced on it. A row without a source is marked as unassessed, not deleted.
3. **Retire `MOB` as an identity.** "Mobility" stays, in the lenses that already hold it: ICF d4 codes and the access needs. Each existing `MOB` row is then judged by what it names, which is what the delegated resolution OD-14 already proposes; this memo adds that for most of the corpus the honest answer is a need and a device, with a community link only where the source names one. The 2026-08-25 ruling is not relitigated here: `WHEEL` is supported by the advocacy evidence (a constituency a membership organisation names). `AMB` is not supported as a self-name; in what I retrieved, "ambulatory" appears as a community word only in "ambulatory wheelchair users", who are exactly the people the binary does not place. That is evidence for the owner, who ruled the split "to start" and has since said `MOB` has more than two levels.
4. **Device in one home.** `device_class` on the extraction is the only place a device is stated. The owner's first two decompositions (aid type; power versus manual) are already that column's vocabulary; it lacks prosthesis, crutches and cane. Whether the list should become a small base registry with a foreign key, so that a prosthesis can be added without a schema change, is a tooling question for a separate change (rule 10).
5. **Impairment level in one home.** Paraplegia versus quadriplegia goes to the medical lens when a source names it (the lens is demand-populated by ruling), and to ICF b-codes for the function it changes. It is not an identity row.
6. **Family as data.** Use `parent_code`, or a typed relation, for families and coalitions: a wheeled-mobility family, the neurodivergent coalition over autistic people, ADHDers and others, body size. A parent is for navigation and for finding commonality "at an abstract level", as the owner's second statement of 2026-11-12 requires. It keys a determination only if it passes the ratified design-equivalence test. Relation types should distinguish "is a coalition that includes" from "is a design-equivalent family of", because `NDV` is the first and `COM` was ratified as the second.
7. **Provenance as its own relation.** Who co-produced a source (the Co-1 warrant) is recorded once, as a link from the source to a community, separate from the lens that says whom a determination serves. The two are different facts; today one code is asked to carry both, and `root_population_note` carries the first in prose.
8. **`ALL` leaves the identity lens** if the owner confirms the hedged 2026-11-06 reading; universal scale is already stated by `design_scale`.
9. **Split the compound cells** (`DEAF`; `LMB`) when their rows are next touched, on the project's own 2026-11-06 schema rule (one value per cell).

**What this does not do.** It does not say which communities to add; that needs a wider and non-English reading of organisations than this pass managed. It does not rank communities. It does not touch `COM` or `BRAIN`, which the owner ratified with eyes open; it only reports that the advocacy lens and the design-equivalence test give different answers for `COM`.

**Confidence and what would change it:** as stated in section 0.
